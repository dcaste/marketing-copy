# Marketing Copywriting Techniques Reference

Research on 63 rhetorical devices and persuasion structures used in headlines, ads, and marketing copy. Each technique includes: description, mechanics (why it works psychologically/linguistically), usage rules, and 10 examples.

> Note on sources: the original list included 3 duplicate techniques (Alliteration, Tricky Threes, Wake-Up Call). They are documented once. Total unique techniques: **63**.

> Note on examples: real, recognizable slogans are mixed with original examples (some built for typical agency client types: restaurants, auto repair shops, beauty salons, event companies) so this works as an applicable reference.

# Usage Guide: When to Apply Each Technique

Best-practice table mapping each technique to **funnel stage**, **ideal channel**, and **risk / when to avoid it**. Built so an agent (or you) can choose the right technique based on context, not just rhetorical effect.

**Funnel stage legend:**
- **Attraction** (TOFU): the goal is to stop the scroll / capture the attention of someone who doesn't know you or isn't actively thinking about buying.
- **Conversion** (MOFU/BOFU): the goal is to move someone already interested toward a purchase decision (handling objections, urgency, comparison).
- **Retention/Branding**: the goal is to build relationship, brand personality, or post-purchase loyalty, not an immediate sale.

**How to read the risk column:** it doesn't mean "don't use this," it means "verify this condition before using it."

**General combination rules (independent of technique):**
- Use a maximum of 1-2 techniques per piece of copy; combining more than 2 dilutes the message and feels overworked.
- For **short headlines** (ads, OOH, subject lines), prioritize Group A and B techniques (sound and contrast) — they perform best in tight spaces.
- For **landing pages and long sales copy**, prioritize Group D techniques (persuasion structures) — they handle objections and urgency better than purely rhetorical devices.
- For **long-term brand/voice building**, prioritize Group C — these define personality, not just a one-off conversion.
- Before using any "high risk" technique (Anomaly, Bash Competitors, Shock Headlines), verify the fact or the legality before publishing, not after.

# A. Sound & Rhythm Devices

## 1. Alliteration

**Rule:** Repeat the opening sound (not just the letter) across 2–4 key benefit words; more than 4 becomes a tongue-twister.

1. Dunkin': "America Runs on Dunkin'."
2. "Crisp cuts, clean fades." (barbershop/salon)

**Risk:** Avoid in long copy or very formal B2B; more than 4 words becomes a tongue-twister

---

## 2. Rhyme

**Rule:** Close two short lines (4–8 syllables each) with matching end sounds; never force a word that distorts the real message.

1. M&M's: "Melts in your mouth, not in your hand."
2. "No reservation? No frustration." (restaurant)

**Risk:** Avoid in serious sectors (health, legal, grief); risk of sounding childish

---

## 3. Onomatopoeia

**Rule:** Use a word that audibly imitates the product's sound or benefit; keep it to one accent per piece and only where the sensory connection is genuine.

1. Rice Krispies: "Snap, Crackle, Pop."
2. "Sizzle now, savor later." (steakhouse)

**Risk:** Avoid in abstract categories (finance, legal, B2B software)

---

## 4. Asyndeton

**Rule:** List 3+ short, strong words or phrases separated only by commas — drop "and" entirely so each word hits on its own.

1. "Power, performance, precision." (Cadillac style)
2. "Diagnosis, repair, warranty." (auto shop)

**Risk:** Don't use if the message needs nuance or explanation

---

## 5. Epistrophe

**Rule:** End exactly 3 consecutive lines with the same word — repeat the word verbatim, not a synonym, and make it the message's central concept.

1. Lincoln (style): "Government of the people, by the people, for the people."
2. "We don't just fix cars. We fix trust. We fix peace of mind." (auto shop)

**Risk:** Loses power in one-line transactional messages

---

## 6. Anaphora

**Rule:** Open exactly 3 consecutive lines with the same literal word or phrase; each line must add a new idea, not restate the same one.

1. "No fine print. No surprises. No excuses."
2. "You deserve an honest shop. You deserve a clear price. You deserve a car that starts." (auto shop)

**Risk:** Same as Epistrophe: not for short, direct messages

---

## 7. Parallelism

**Rule:** Match the exact grammatical structure (same part of speech, same word count) across 2–3 blocks; never mix verb tenses between blocks.

1. "Less waiting, more trust." (auto shop)
2. "Less guessing, more data." (agency)

**Risk:** Low risk; watch for mixed verb tenses between blocks

---

## 8. Chiasmus

**Rule:** Take two concepts (A-B) and reverse their order in the second phrase (B-A) so the reversal reveals a truth — only use it if it comes out naturally; never force it.

1. JFK (style): "Ask not what your country can do for you, ask what you can do for your country."
2. "We don't earn your trust to take care of your car. We take care of your car to earn your trust." (auto shop)

**Risk:** If it doesn't come out naturally, don't force it — use another technique

---

## 9. Elision

**Rule:** Remove letters or words so what's missing is instantly obvious to reconstruct — one elision per ad, always paired with a visual design element (cut-off text, strikethrough).

1. "Less talk. More ac—" (with "action" cut off visually)
2. "Car ready, no 'but—'." (auto shop)

**Risk:** Only works visually; don't use on radio/audio

---

## 10. Break It Up

**Rule:** Find a smaller meaningful word hidden inside a larger one, then use capitalization or color to visually surface it — the hidden word must reinforce the message, not just be a disconnected trick.

1. "rePAIR shop you can trust." (auto shop)
2. "EVENTual perfection." (event company)

**Risk:** Visual only; fully design-dependent

---

## 11. Typographic Simile

**Rule:** Replace exactly one letter in a key word with a visual object that both resembles that letter in shape and represents the core product or benefit — one substitution per word, max.

1. Toblerone: the "O" replaced by a triangular mountain.
2. Auto shop: the "O" in "MOTOR" as a tire.

**Risk:** Visual only; requires graphic design, doesn't apply to pure copy

---

## 12. Text Highlights

**Rule:** Write a natural-sounding full sentence, then highlight only the words that, read alone, form a complete second message — use a single highlight style per ad.

1. "Your car deserves a shop that **won't lie to you**." (auto shop)
2. "We design campaigns that **sell, not just decorate**." (agency)

**Risk:** Doesn't work in audio; needs layout/design

---

# B. Meaning & Contrast Devices

## 13. Antithesis

**Rule:** Pair two clear, instantly recognizable opposites in a short, symmetrical line — same word count on each pole when possible.

1. Apple M1: "Small chip. Giant leap."
2. "Small shop, big solutions." (auto repair)

**Risk:** Low risk; very versatile

---

## 14. Oxymoron

**Rule:** Fuse two contradictory terms into one tight expression (adjective + noun is the classic form) so the contradiction resolves into a coherent new meaning — one oxymoron per piece, max.

1. "Fast food, made slow." (casual restaurant)
2. "Instant repair, lifetime warranty." (auto shop)

**Risk:** One per piece max; more than one feels forced

---

## 15. Metaphor

**Rule:** Declare the product "is" something else with universal emotional resonance — no "like" or "as." Never mix two different metaphors in the same sentence.

1. Red Bull: "Gives you wings."
2. "This menu is a trip to Italy without a passport." (restaurant)

**Risk:** Don't mix two different metaphors in the same message

---

## 16. Personification

**Rule:** Give the product (or its enemy) one clear human action verb that reveals its personality — the verb does the work, not a long description.

1. Oreo: "Milk's favorite cookie."
2. "This engine doesn't know what tired means." (auto shop)

**Risk:** Avoid on serious or sensitive topics (don't personify a severe illness)

---

## 17. Analogy

**Rule:** Keep "like" or "as" visible and compare to a universally known everyday experience — one analogy per message, never turn it into a half-metaphor.

1. "Our service is like having a mechanic you trust living on your block." (auto shop)
2. "A campaign without data is like driving at night with no headlights." (agency)

**Risk:** Low risk; ideal for simplifying complex concepts

---

## 18. Allusion

**Rule:** Reference something culturally recognizable to your exact audience and never explain it — if the reference needs clarification, it didn't work.

1. "Michelangelo's David took 3 years. Your website, 3 weeks." (agency)
2. "A wedding worthy of Cinderella, minus the midnight scare." (events)

**Risk:** Cultural/generational risk if the reference isn't known; watch trademark issues

---

## 19. Pun

**Rule:** Take a very well-known phrase and swap exactly one word for a near-identical sound-alike that connects directly to the product — never use a pun that requires explanation.

1. "No more breakdowns, just brake-throughs." (auto shop)
2. "The cut above the rest." (salon/barbershop)

**Risk:** Avoid in serious sectors (severe health, legal, grief)

---

## 20. Double Meaning

**Rule:** Choose one well-known ambiguous word where both its literal (product-level) and figurative (emotional) meanings are simultaneously true — build the line around that single word.

1. "We have the best brakes in the market. And in the city." (auto shop)
2. "Fast connection, in every sense." (telecom)

**Risk:** Low risk if both meanings land equally clearly

---

## 21. Pleonasm

**Rule:** Stack 2–3 synonyms or qualifiers for the single most important benefit in a deliberate, rhythmic list — one rhetorical redundancy per piece, max.

1. "Free, no cost, no commitment." (offer)
2. "One of a kind, exclusive, unrepeatable." (events)

**Risk:** Max one rhetorical redundancy per piece

---

## 22. Flipped Phrase

**Rule:** Take a highly recognizable saying and replace exactly one word with its antonym — leave everything else untouched so the familiar structure makes the swap land.

1. "Better late than never... except when it comes to hot food." (restaurant)
2. "He who hesitates is lost. She who plans, wins."

**Risk:** Low risk

---

## 23. Rearrange

**Rule:** Reorder the same 3–6 words so the new sequence genuinely shifts meaning or priority — if the reordering only confuses, revert.

1. "Your car, before your schedule." (auto shop)
2. "Results, then budget. Not the other way around." (agency)

**Risk:** Risk of confusion if the new order isn't clear on first read

---

## 24. Reverse World

**Rule:** Flip the expected power hierarchy (who waits for whom, who chases whom) so the inversion is absurd at first glance but resolves into a credible benefit within two seconds.

1. "Here, you don't wait for the mechanic. The mechanic waits for you." (auto shop)
2. "You don't look for customers. Customers find you." (agency/SEO)

**Risk:** Risk of sounding absurd if the inversion doesn't make sense after a 2-second think

---

## 25. Reverse Psychology

**Rule:** Say "don't do this" about what you want them to do, paired with a short credible reason — the tone must sound genuine, not sarcastic, and never lie about real availability.

1. "Don't come to this shop if you like waiting 3 days for a checkup." (auto shop)
2. "Don't hire us if your event doesn't deserve to be unforgettable." (events)

**Risk:** Don't use to lie about real availability; avoid in serious sectors

---

# C. Tone, Voice & Brand Personality

## 26. Self-Aware Ads

**Rule:** Name the ad in the very first line, then immediately deliver real value or a real laugh — the admission alone without a payoff is just cynicism.

1. "Yes, this is an ad. But one worth reading."
2. "This is an ad for a restaurant. And you'll still be hungry by the end of it."

**Risk:** Avoid in sectors requiring absolute seriousness (health, legal)

---

## 27. Self-Deprecation

**Rule:** Admit a real, minor, non-dealbreaker flaw in a light tone, then immediately follow with a strong benefit that more than compensates.

1. Avis: "We're number two, so we try harder."
2. "We're not the newest shop in town. We're the one that won't lie to you." (auto shop)

**Risk:** Avoid where technical trust is critical (medical, legal, security)

---

## 28. Understatement

**Rule:** Describe something objectively large with a deliberately casual, almost bored tone — the gap between real scale and modest language is the whole joke. Never pair with exclamation marks.

1. "We cut hair, I guess." (a salon with a months-long waitlist)
2. "Some people say it's good coffee." (a café with a daily line out the door)

**Risk:** Requires an audience that picks up on irony; risk of falling flat with literal audiences

---

## 29. Humblebrag

**Rule:** Frame the impressive fact as a casual question that contains the brag inside it — the fact must be real and verifiable, and use it once per piece only.

1. "Is it weird that people drive 40 minutes just to eat here?" (restaurant)
2. "Weird that 3 big brands called us this week without us reaching out first?" (agency)

**Risk:** The fact must be real and verifiable; if false, it damages credibility

---

## 30. Shock Headlines

**Rule:** Open with a surprising or provocative claim, then immediately follow with context that justifies it — the shock must connect directly to the real message, never be offensive or discriminatory, and be used sparingly.

1. "Your mechanic is overcharging you. And they probably don't even realize it." (auto shop)
2. "Your website is costing you customers every minute it stays like this." (agency)

**Risk:** High risk: never offensive or discriminatory; use sparingly

---

## 31. Dialogue

**Rule:** Use the exact words your real customer would say — in quotation marks — and make it specific enough that the right reader thinks "that's exactly what I'd say."

1. "'Can you make it look like the photo?' Yes. Always yes." (salon)
2. "'Why can't anyone find my business on Google?' That's exactly why we exist." (agency)

**Risk:** Low risk

---

## 32. Said No One Ever

**Rule:** Open with an absurd quote that literally describes the pain your product solves, then close with "said no one ever" — the closing phrase always goes at the end, never the start.

1. "'I love waiting 3 hours to get my car checked,' said no one ever." (auto shop)
2. "'I hope the DJ doesn't show up to my wedding,' said no one ever." (events)

**Risk:** Avoid with serious or sensitive pain points (health, grief, loss)

---

## 33. Imperative

**Rule:** Open with a short, concrete action verb and immediately pair it with the benefit — one imperative per sentence, motivating in tone, never authoritarian.

1. Nike: "Just Do It."
2. "Stop guessing. Start measuring." (agency)

**Risk:** Low risk; the base of almost every CTA

---

## 34. Observations

**Rule:** State a specific, concrete human truth that sounds like something an observant friend would say — keep it short, don't force a sales close immediately after it.

1. "Nobody checks the oil until the car's already on the tow truck." (auto shop)
2. "Nobody remembers a wedding's menu. Everyone remembers whether there was chaos or not." (events)

**Risk:** Low risk

---

## 35. A Different Breed

**Rule:** Use "those who know / people who get it" language to elevate the right reader into a select group — never directly belittle those who don't buy, and always give a credible reason why that group "knows more."

1. "It's not for every car. It's for the ones whose owners actually care." (auto shop)
2. "This isn't generic marketing. It's for brands that already get that data calls the shots." (agency)

**Risk:** Don't directly belittle those who don't buy

---

# D. Persuasion Structures (Copy Frameworks)

## 36. Reveals

**Rule:** Start with a genuinely predictable phrase, then place the twist at the very last word — the twist must connect logically to the product, and the whole thing must stay to one short sentence.

1. "Nothing beats home-cooked food... until you try ours." (restaurant)
2. "Rome wasn't built in a day... your new website will be." (agency)

**Risk:** Low risk

---

## 37. Tricky Threes

**Rule:** Set a clear pattern with the first two elements, then break it with a third that's shorter, more pointed, and logically unexpected — exactly three elements, never two or four.

1. "We change oil, we change tires, we change your mind about auto shops." (auto shop)
2. "Cut, color, confidence." (salon)

**Risk:** Low risk

---

## 38. Wake-Up Call

**Rule:** Name a specific, credible cost (time, money, concrete risk) that is ongoing right now — then follow immediately with the solution; a wake-up call with no exit creates anxiety, not action.

1. "Every week without analytics is a week of flying blind." (agency)
2. "Every vendor without a signed contract is a risk your wedding is taking." (events)

**Risk:** Don't exaggerate the cost to the point of sounding alarmist or false

---

## 39. Objections

**Rule:** Use the customer's real, exact-language doubt as the headline, then answer it with a concrete fact or guarantee — never dodge the strongest objection to address a weaker one.

1. "'They're probably going to inflate the price.' Nope. Diagnosis is free and in writing." (auto shop)
2. "'It's probably just another agency overpromising.' That's why we show you real client results before you pay a cent." (agency)

**Risk:** Low risk; high persuasive value

---

## 40. Concierge

**Rule:** Hand the reader a specific, credible "gift" (time saved, money recovered) as a done deal, then ask what they'll do with it — never answer the question for them.

1. "We saved you 2 hours of waiting at the shop. What would you do with them?"
2. "You no longer have to think about event logistics. What are you going to think about instead?" (events)

**Risk:** Low risk

---

## 41. What If?

**Rule:** Keep the "What if...?" question short and specific to one concrete, easy-to-imagine desirable scenario — follow it with the statement that this scenario is now real, thanks to the product.

1. "What if your car never left you stranded on the road again?" (auto shop)
2. "What if your business showed up first every time someone searched for it?" (agency)

**Risk:** Low risk

---

## 42. Slap 'n Hug

**Rule:** Deliver a real, non-exaggerated uncomfortable truth in one short sentence (the slap), then follow immediately with a warm solution in the next sentence (the hug) — the end tone must feel warm, not sarcastic.

1. "Your business is invisible online without you even knowing it. We make you visible where it matters." (agency)
2. "Your hair reflects how you treat yourself. Let us help you treat it better." (salon)

**Risk:** The "slap" shouldn't sound aggressive or alarmist

---

## 43. Challenge

**Rule:** Make the dare concrete with a measurable outcome and always pair it with a low-risk way to accept (guarantee, free trial, no commitment) — confident in tone, never arrogant.

1. "I dare you to find a more honest shop in this city. If you do, let us know." (auto shop)
2. "Pit your current website against our strategy for 30 days. The numbers speak for themselves." (agency)

**Risk:** Low risk if you offer a low-risk way to accept (guarantee, free trial)

---

## 44. Call the Bluff

**Rule:** Ask the uncomfortable question the whole industry avoids, then answer it yourself honestly and immediately — the practice you call out must be real, not invented.

1. "Why don't most auto shops show you the parts they actually replaced? We do." (auto shop)
2. "Why don't most agencies share the real results of their campaigns? We publish ours." (agency)

**Risk:** The practice called out must be real and verifiable, not invented

---

## 45. Number Juxtaposition

**Rule:** Place two real, verifiable numbers side by side so the contrast is immediately obvious without requiring mental math — one numeric comparison per message, max.

1. "Other shops: 3 days. Us: 3 hours." (auto shop)
2. "Typical result: 3 months to see change. Ours: 3 weeks." (agency)

**Risk:** Figures must be real and verifiable

---

## 46. Social Math

**Rule:** Translate an abstract large number into one universally known everyday unit in a single short sentence — verify the math is accurate, not misleadingly rounded.

1. "The time we saved our clients this year adds up to 400 full days." (agency)
2. "The wait time we eliminated this year equals 60 full days of life given back to our customers." (auto shop)

**Risk:** The math must be honest, not misleading

---

## 47. Activation

**Rule:** Ask the reader to do one simple, immediate action they can perform right in front of the ad, then connect that experience directly to the product's benefit — one action only, never complex instructions.

1. "Count to 10. That's how fast we respond at our shop." (auto shop)
2. "Count how many scrolls it took you to find your brand online today. We remove that step." (agency)

**Risk:** Doesn't work well on radio (depends on exposure time)

---

## 48. Real-Time

**Rule:** Tie directly to a current event with a genuine, instantly obvious connection — publish immediately while the moment is live; the news hook is the entry point, the brand message must follow just as fast.

1. "Did you see gas prices jump this week? That's why we check your fuel consumption for free." (auto shop)
2. "The social algorithm changed again this week. We've already adapted our clients' strategy." (agency)

**Risk:** Requires fast publishing; avoid tragedies, polarizing politics, or sensitive topics

---

## 49. Just for Jim

**Rule:** Name one hyper-specific profile (role + concrete situation + habit) in the headline so the right reader feels personally "read" — accept that everyone else feels excluded; that exclusion is what makes it credible.

1. "For the business owner who pays for ads and doesn't know if they're working." (agency)
2. "For the couple who switched event venues twice because nobody understood their vision." (events)

**Risk:** Excludes the general audience — that's intentional, not a flaw

---

## 50. Anomaly

**Rule:** Claim "the best/only/first" within a narrowly defined, provable category — the more specific the category, the more credible the superlative; never use it if you can't back it up under a direct question.

1. "The only shop in the area certified for electronic diagnostics on the country's top 3 best-selling brands." (auto shop)
2. "The first event company to include rain insurance in the base package." (events)

**Risk:** High risk: the claim must be 100% provable (legal/credibility risk if not)

---

## 51. Time to Value

**Rule:** State a specific result + a specific, consistently achievable deadline in the same sentence — back it with a guarantee if the timeframe is aggressive, never use vague words like "fast" or "soon."

1. "A full diagnostic of your car in 20 minutes, or the check is free." (auto shop)
2. "First measurable results from your campaign in 14 days." (agency)

**Risk:** The deadline must be consistently achievable, not just in the best case

---

## 52. Something for Someone

**Rule:** Use the structure [what it is] + "for" + [hyper-specific audience by role/situation], not a generic demographic label — the specificity must reflect real understanding of that audience's need.

1. "Digital strategy for local businesses that have never run paid ads before." (agency)
2. "Wedding package for couples planning everything remotely from another city." (events)

**Risk:** Low risk

---

# E. Positioning, Pain & Competition

## 53. Enemy

**Rule:** Declare war on a concept, habit, or industry practice the audience already genuinely resents — never name a real person or competing brand — then immediately back it with proof you fight that enemy.

1. "We declared war on made-up diagnostics." (auto shop)
2. "We declared war on advertising nobody can measure." (agency)

**Risk:** Keep it a concept or habit, not a named person or competing brand

---

## 54. Bash Competitors

**Rule:** Attack the competitor's practice or result — never their character — using humor or irony to soften it; verify local comparative-advertising law before naming them by name.

1. "Other shops replace parts 'just in case.' We only replace what actually failed." (auto shop)
2. "Other agencies hand you a 40-page report nobody reads. We call you and explain it in 10 minutes." (agency)

**Risk:** High risk: check local comparative-advertising law before naming competitors

---

## 55. Legal Loopholes

**Rule:** State a real regulatory limitation, briefly explain the "why," then immediately turn it into proof of honesty by contrasting it with what you can guarantee.

1. "By law, we can't guarantee your car will never have an issue again. But we can guarantee the work we do on it." (auto shop)
2. "We can't guarantee the #1 spot on Google (no honest agency can). We can guarantee the work it takes to genuinely try." (agency)

**Risk:** Low risk if the shared limitation is real, not an excuse

---

## 56. Downsides

**Rule:** Confess one real, plausible "problem of abundance" in a single ironic warning line — the downside must be desirable, never a mask for a genuinely negative product flaw.

1. "Warning: people will ask you 3 times a day where you got your hair done." (salon)
2. "Warning: you're going to start noticing how weak your competitor's marketing is." (agency)

**Risk:** Low risk if the tone is humorous and the "downside" is desirable

---

## 57. Benefit of the Benefit

**Rule:** State the direct functional benefit, then chain it to its deeper consequence with "so" or "which means" — stop at two levels; a third level confuses.

1. "We fix your car fast, so you don't lose your whole workday." (auto shop)
2. "We boost your Google ranking, so you stop paying for ads you no longer need." (agency)

**Risk:** Low risk; don't chain more than 2 benefit levels

---

## 58. Chuck Norris

**Rule:** Exaggerate to an obviously impossible level so no one mistakes it for a real promise — keep the connection to the real benefit behind the joke clear, and the tone always playful, never serious.

1. "Our mechanic doesn't go looking for the problem. The problem finds him, and surrenders on the spot." (auto shop)
2. "Our ads don't go looking for customers. Customers find the ads and give up resisting on the spot." (agency)

**Risk:** Only for brands with a relaxed tone; avoid in serious sectors

---

## 59. Stop the Pain

**Rule:** Open with "Stop," "Skip," or "Goodbye to" as the literal first word, name a specific recognizable pain, then present the solution immediately without dwelling on the problem.

1. "Stop paying for repairs you never needed." (auto shop)
2. "Goodbye to campaigns nobody knows are even working." (agency)

**Risk:** Don't exaggerate the pain to the point of sounding manipulative

---

## 60. Tired of Pain?

**Rule:** Open with "Tired of" + a repetitive, ongoing frustration in the customer's own exact language — follow directly with the solution, without dragging out the description of the fatigue.

1. "Tired of being charged for things nobody even explains to you?" (auto shop)
2. "Tired of paying for ads without knowing if they're actually working?" (agency)

**Risk:** Same risk as Stop the Pain: don't overdramatize

---

## 61. Goal, Not Pain

**Rule:** Always open with the positive desired result, then use "not" to briefly cross out the old way in the middle — the negation is a quick contrast, never the closer.

1. "Customers who actually convert, not just visits that leave nothing behind." (agency)
2. "The event you dreamed of, not the one you end up running on pure exhaustion." (events)

**Risk:** Low risk

---

## 62. Gain Without Pain

**Rule:** Use the structure [desired result] + "without" + [the most obvious expected sacrifice] — the "without" promise must be genuinely deliverable, not misleading.

1. "More customers, without blowing your whole ad budget." (agency)
2. "The wedding of your dreams, without fighting with your partner over logistics." (events)

**Risk:** The "without sacrifice" promise must be deliverable, not misleading

---

## 63. This Into That

**Rule:** Use an explicit transformation word ("we turn," "from... into...") with a concrete, immediately recognizable "before" state and a concrete desired "after" state — keep both ends short.

1. "We turn your followers into customers who actually buy." (agency)
2. "We turn your wedding to-do list into a single confirmation call." (events)

**Risk:** Low risk
